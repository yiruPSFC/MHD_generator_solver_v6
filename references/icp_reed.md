# Induction-Coupled Plasma Torch 

Thomas B. Reed<br>Lincoln Laboratory,* Massachusells Institute of Technology, Lexington 73, Massachusetts

(Received October 4, 1960)


#### Abstract

A new method of generating a stable plasma at atmospheric pressure using inductive coupling at a frequency of several Mc is described. Methods of starting and operating this plasma in argon, and mixtures of argon with helium, hydrogen, oxygen, and air are discussed. The Fowler and Milne method was used to measure the temperature profile of the plasma under various conditions of gas flow and composition, and at several power levels. Measured peak temperatures ranged from $14000^{\circ}-19000^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$. The power losses from the plasma in the form of convection, radiation, and conduction to the nozzle walls were measured under the same conditions. Total power transferred to the plasma ranged from $1.6-3.1 \mathrm{kw}$ which was approximately $50 \%$ of the input power. The extent to which local thermal equilibrium prevails in the plasma is discussed; the available evidence indicates that under the operating conditions described herein, equilibrium is closely approached.


## INTRODUCTION

THIS article describes a plasma torch based on inductive coupling to an ionized gas at or near thermal equilibrium. Since the torch is electrodeless and can be used with any gas desired, this type of apparatus should be useful in high temperature research and engineering. Operation is extremely simple.

A plasma torch is a device designed to heat gases to very high temperatures by taking advantage of the high conductivity of an ionized gas. Conventional plasma torches require electrodes to carry energy to the gas; they operate on direct current carried by thermionic or high-field cathodes and water-cooled anode nozzles. ${ }^{1-3}$ Because of the high conductivity of partially ionized gases [e.g., the conductivity of argon at $15000^{\circ} \mathrm{K}^{4}$ is about $120(\text { ohm } \mathrm{cm})^{-1}$ ] these torches are characterized by a high current ( $100-1000 \mathrm{amp}$ ) and a low voltage $(10-100 \mathrm{v})$. The gases in the arc of these electrodecoupled plasma torches probably approach local thermal equilibrium (see below). These electrode-coupled dc plasma torches are limited in their operation because the electrodes are subject to attack by such gases as oxygen and nitrogen.

A second type of coupling scheme is encountered in the microwave torch ${ }^{5}$; here, advantage is taken of the high-field breakdown at the end of a coaxial tube to dissociate diatomic gases. Thermal equilibrium is probably not achieved in this device, since heating effects are attributed to reassociation of diatomic molecules, while monatomic gases, though luminous, transfer very little heat.

Although the inductively coupled plasma torch is new, high-pressure inductive discharges were previously

[^0]observed by Babat. ${ }^{6}$ While working with low-pressure discharges he was able to observe, as the pressure was increased, a transition to what appears to have been a thermal plasma in air at very high power levels ( 100 kw). No effort was made to flow gases through the discharge to make a plasma torch.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INDUCTION PLASMA TORCH

In its simplest form, the induction plasma torch is a quartz tube, open at one end, with gas supplied at the other end (see Fig. 1). An rf coil of a few turns surrounds the tube at the center and serves to couple energy into the plasma. Most of the work reported in this paper has been carried out using a cylindrical fiveturn induction coil of $\frac{3}{16}-\mathrm{in}$. copper tubing, with a spacing of $\frac{5}{16}$ in. between turns. However, it is observed experimentally that the plasma is equally stable and more easily visible, using a flat five-turn (pancake) coil. The power was supplied to the coil by a commercial (Lepel) rf heating unit with a maximum power output of 10 kw . The plasma was operated at a frequency of 4 Mc .

The operation of the induction-coupled plasma torch, though simple, depends on proper starting procedures, stabilization methods, and gas composition.

## Starting

In early experiments, in order to form the main plasma, a small "pilot" plasma was formed upstream (relative to the rf coil) using a conventional $30-\mathrm{amp}$, 60 -cycle ac arc, operated in argon between two watercooled tungsten electrodes inclined at 5 degrees to the tube axis. The rf coil readily coupled to the pilot plasma thus formed, immediately enlarging it into the main plasma. It was later found that the coupling could be initiated by heating a graphite rod or refractory wire loop inductively within the rf field and then withdrawing it. ## Stabilization

Once the plasma has been formed, it must propagate rapidly against the gas flow in the rf field since the plasma is continuously being swept away by the gas flow; otherwise, the plasma extinguishes in a manner similar to unstabilized flames. At low gas velocity, ordinary heat conduction can maintain the plasma but at higher velocity, steps must be taken to recirculate some of the plasma. The most satisfactory solution found so far is vortex stabilization, which is accomplished by feeding the gas into the tube tangentially, which causes it to flow spirally down the walls. This creates a low-pressure area in the center of the tube, which causes some of the plasma to flow up the tube countercurrent to the main flow. With this type of stabilization, the visible plasma extends farther upstream, the greater the gas flow. Also, the flow along the walls tends to keep the plasma centered and away from the walls and tends to cool the tube walls, making it possible to operate at higher power levels. Several other methods of plasma stabilization have been found effective. For instance, the gas was injected from a small-diameter tube at the axis of the large tube above the plasma region. The aspiration of gas into the jet thus formed causes a recirculation which has also been found to result in good stabilization.

## Gas Composition

Argon is the simplest gas in which to start and operate the plasma, due probably to its low heat capacity at ionizing temperatures and due to its poor thermal conductivity. For this reason the plasma torch was always started in pure argon. The plasma has been operated in argon, and argon mixtures containing $20 \%$ air, helium, or hydrogen, or 50\% oxygen. Under these conditions the plasma contracts and the power consumption increases.

It is probable that the induction plasma torch can be operated in any of these pure gases, provided enough power is supplied and cooling of the tube walls is adequate.

## EXPERIMENTAL <br> Plasma Temperature Measurement

The temperature of the plasma was measured as a function of radial ( $r$ ) and axial ( $z$ ) position, power level, gas flow, and gas composition using the method of Fowler and Milne ${ }^{7}$ as applied to transparent arc plasmas by Larenz ${ }^{8}$ and more rigorously used by H. N. Olsen ${ }^{9}$ to establish the existence of thermal equilibrium in dc arc plasmas. This method depends on the fact that the intensity of a given spectral line of an element from a gas in thermal equilibrium will increase with temperature, and then reach a maximum, falling off at higher temperatures due to depopulation of the ground state when thermal ionization becomes extensive (see Fig. 2). If a gas is in thermal equilibrium, the intensity of a given spectral line due to spontaneous transitions from an excited $E_{m}$ state to a lower level is given as a function of temperature by

$$
i(T)=K \frac{n_{0}(T)}{u_{0}(T)} \exp \left(-E_{m l} / k T\right),
$$

where $n_{0}$ is the number density of atoms in the ground state (obtained from the Saha equation), $u_{0}$ is the internal partition function for the atom, and the constant $K$ depends on the quantum energy, transition probability, and statistical weight of the upper state. The above equation is based on the assumption that thermal equilibrium prevails; the question as to whether this is the case in the operation of the plasma torch will be discussed below.

## DISCUSSION

## Thermal Equilibrium

The temperature measurements given above are only valid if local thermal equilibrium exists in the plasma. No direct tests have been carried out to determine whether this is the case, but there are a number of facts which suggest that the plasma cannot be very far from equilibrium.

In a plasma at atmospheric pressure, the mean free path of the electrons is very short and the collision rate very high, so that it is difficult for the electrons to acquire energies much in excess of those of the atoms and ions. Thus the field strength provides an approximate test for the difference between particle and electron energies. This difference (in equivalent temperature) is given by ${ }^{4,9}$

$$
\Delta T / T=M\left(e \lambda_{e} E^{2}\right) /\left[4 m(3 k T / 2)^{2}\right]
$$

where $\Delta T$ is the difference between electron and mean gas temperatures at an average temperature $T ; M$ and $m$ are the masses of the atoms and electrons; $\lambda_{e}$ is the electron mean free path, and $E$ is the local field strength.

The local field strength in the induction plasma can be estimated from the properties of the plasma. The skin depth at these frequencies, assuming a conductivity of 100 (ohm cm) ${ }^{-1}$ is 0.25 cm . In plasma 1 of Table I , the eddy field strength is calculated to be about 1.6
$\mathrm{v} / \mathrm{cm}$ from the total power input to the plasma and the geometry of the conduction region. Substituting this in the above equation yields a value of $\Delta T / T$ of about $10^{-4}$. In dc plasmas, Olsen ${ }^{9}$ has measured $\Delta T / T$ values of 0.001 and 0.2 near the anode and cathode, respectively.

Olsen has examined the thermal equilibrium question experimentally in dc arc plasmas by measuring atom, ion, ${ }^{9}$ and electron ${ }^{12}$ temperatures at the same position in the arc. The temperatures are all found to agree to better than $10 \%$. The induction plasma, while generated in an entirely different way, has many properties in common with the de plasma. A typical dc plasma in a $200-\mathrm{amp}$ argon arc has a cross section near the anode of 12 mm , a peak temperature of $15000^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$, a convective flow of $5 \mathrm{l} / \mathrm{min}$ (NTP) of gas carrying 2.4 kw of thermal energy due to streaming of the gas caused by selfmagnetic forces in the cathode region. ${ }^{13}$ These parameters are very similar to those of the induction plasmas in Table I.

Finally, the gas flows and energy transfer to the plasma shown in Table I define an average temperature, $\bar{T}$ of the gas flowing through the tube, based on the heat capacity and ionization energies of the gases in question. These average temperatures are shown in the last column of Table I. While these values are considerably below the peak plasma temperatures, it must be remembered that a disproportionate fraction (due to higher density) of the gas flows along the relatively cool walls, so that the temperature profiles are consistent with the average temperatures shown.

Although none of the above facts prove that the induction plasma is in thermal equilibrium, taken together they suggest that it is not very far from equilibrium.

## Utility

It is still too early to say what the capabilities and the limitations of inductive plasma generation are. We have used the plasma torch to grow single crystals ${ }^{14}$ of refractory oxides, and the oxygen-containing plasma will melt zirconium oxide ( $\mathrm{mp}=2700^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$ ). The temperatures achieved in the plasma will depend ultimately on the power available and the nondestructive confinement of the plasma. The heat transfer attainable will depend on the velocities and temperatures of the plasma. Presumably, the induction plasma will find use wherever the dc plasmas are used, from measurement of spectral transition probabilities to preheating gases for use in hypersonic wind tunnels.
